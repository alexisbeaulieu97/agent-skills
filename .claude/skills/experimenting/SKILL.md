# Experimenting

Find out how options compare from what happens in practice, not from what
theory, habit or reputation predicts. A belief that one option works better
feels like knowledge and is often wrong, and it stays wrong for as long as
nobody tries the other. When a claim about how options compare can be tried
and is worth trying, try it, and let numbers and observations carry the
result rather than impressions. The aim is a result grounded in reality, at a
cost in proportion to what rests on it.

## Spot the claim

A comparison worth testing usually hides in a claim about what works better or
what will happen: that one option is slower, that people will not read
something that long, that a split will be easier to look after, that a plan
fits the time. Treat such a claim as a prediction until something observed
backs it, whoever makes it: you, another agent, a rule of thumb or common
knowledge. The evidence may already exist, such as a measurement, a record or
someone's direct experience of this very case, so look before you build one.
Watch most closely when a claim is about to rule an option out: a rejected
option is rarely tried again, so a wrong rejection stays hidden.

## When an experiment is worth it

An experiment is worth running when all three hold:

- **Trying can settle it.** Something you can measure, try or watch gives the
  answer: which version is faster, which wording gets the reply, whether the
  schedule holds, whether the recipe survives doubling. A choice that turns on
  judgement is tried differently; see below.
- **The result could change the choice.** If every outcome, including the one
  you doubt, would lead to the same option, an experiment has nothing to tell.
- **Being wrong costs more than finding out.** Weigh how hard the choice is to
  undo, and how much will be built on it, against the experiment's cost. A
  choice that is cheap to reverse can be corrected later; one that others will
  depend on, or that rules options out, earns an experiment even when the
  answer seems clear.

When all three hold, feeling sure is not a reason to skip one: the
obvious-seeming claims are the ones nobody checks.

## Design it to tell the options apart

- **Count the way things are done now among the options**, or doing nothing
  new. It is the option most often left out, and the bar every other option
  has to clear to be worth its cost; where it comes close, it also shows why
  the others win or lose.
- **State, before trying, which result favours which option**, including what
  holds when no threshold is met: "the second only if it is at least twice as
  fast, otherwise the simpler first". A criterion fixed first cannot drift to
  fit the result. Anchor a relative threshold to the current way, which stays
  put, not to the best option, which changes as options join or drop out and
  can flip the result with it.
- **Test where the options differ, and only that.** Build the smallest thing
  that exercises the point of difference, rather than carrying out each option
  in full. Include where each option should come out ahead, not only where it
  should cost most: a comparison that tries an option only at its weak points
  has decided before it starts.
- **Match the conditions the answer depends on**: realistic size, the real
  people, tools or setting where they could change the outcome. Where the
  experiment differs from the real conditions, count that in the result,
  with whether it could flip it.
- **Include the edges when others will build on the choice**: limits, things
  that run out, the worst pattern of use and what long use does. Ordinary use
  rarely tells such options apart; the facts that decide often sit at the
  edges.
- **Give each option its best fair shot, then freeze it.** Same inputs, same
  conditions, each done the way someone who favoured it would do it, and
  settled before trying. Where an option has to be used to show what it is
  like, its best shot includes one round of that use: each option gets one
  revision for what the use ran into, the same for every option and counted
  in the result, and only then freezes. Afterwards, change an option only to
  fix a mistake, and count that in the result.
- **Have the criterion and the setup checked before running**, by someone
  who did not design them, such as a fresh agent. A bias in the setup, such
  as inputs arranged one way for one option and another way for the rest,
  flatters an option, and checking only the result will not show it.
- **Let numbers speak where there are numbers.** Measure rather than judge by
  feel. Repeat where you can and compare the difference with how much repeated
  tries vary: a difference inside that variation is no difference. Where you
  cannot repeat, treat a small difference as none.
- **Don't let a proxy decide what it stands for.** A count, a timing or a
  score often stands in for something broader, such as how easy the result is
  to work with, and optimising the stand-in can pick the worse option. Use it
  as evidence about the broader thing, not as the verdict.
- **Check the result measured what it claims** before trusting it, most of
  all when it surprises or pleases you.
- **Observe the thing itself.** As illustrations, not a menu: timing both
  versions of a routine, sending two wordings to a few people each, rehearsing
  an agenda with a clock, cooking a half batch, walking the route at the hour
  it will be used.

## Keep it safe and bounded

Run experiments where they cannot change anything that matters: a copy, a
sandbox, a rehearsal, a small trial. One that would reach people, spend money
or alter something shared is a real action, under whatever rules govern that
action, however small the trial.

Keep experiments apart from the work, so a trial is not mistaken for the
work.

Fix a budget before starting, in proportion to what rests on the result. When
the experiment grows towards the cost of the real thing, or the result stays
unclear, stop: an inconclusive result is a finding, that the options do not
differ enough on this point to tell apart.

## Read the result for what it is

A result may not favour one option the way the criterion planned. Read it for
what it does show. As illustrations: each option wins on a different point,
which is a trade-off rather than a winner; every option fails, which may mean
the choice is the wrong question; or the comparison cannot be run here, for
lack of access, people or a setting, in which case what would settle it is the
finding, and any choice made without it is unverified.

A result that shows why an option wins or loses can point to an option nobody
tried. It can join the comparison under the same criterion, not one fitted to
it, counted in the result as having joined after the first results.

A change made to the winner after the comparison is such an option too, when
it could bear on the criterion: a refinement the result suggested, a limit
added for safety, a detail settled afterwards. The result does not cover it
until it has been tried under the same criterion; until then it is untried,
and the result says so.

A result can also reveal a criterion nobody set beforehand. Read the result
both with and without it, and count it in the result as having emerged: if the
outcome is the same either way, the new criterion supports it; if it flips,
the result is a trade-off between the criterion set first and the one that
emerged. The two kinds weigh differently. A criterion revealed by a failure,
such as an option that goes wrong when two things happen at once, is a
requirement that went unstated, and it holds even when found late. A new
preference that happens to favour the option already ahead is the kind most
likely to be fitting the criterion to the result.

## When the choice turns on judgement

Some choices turn on judgement rather than on anything measured. Make each
option just far enough to be seen, alike in everything but the point in
question, so it is judged as the real thing rather than from a description.
Then fix the procedure before trying, rather than a definition of better: who
judges, what they see, that each judges independently without knowing whose
option is whose, the one open question they answer, and how clear a
preference must be to change what exists. Their reasons show what better
meant in this case, including what nobody thought to list.

Ask separately, after the verdict, what change to each option would most
change their preference, so that suggesting does not colour judging. A
refinement tried from that joins late, under the same procedure, and is
judged by people who did not suggest it, since suggesting a change gives a
stake in it.

## What a result is made of

A result is the evidence, not only its verdict: what was compared, the
criterion set beforehand, the conditions, what came out in numbers or
observations, what the experiment does not show, and what it takes to repeat
it, so it can be checked rather than trusted. "B is better" alone cannot be
told apart from a guess.
